The Accordion's roof is in better shape than anyone expected, and in worse shape than it looks. The shell itself is sound. What fails is the edges: the valleys where water sits, and the lips of the folds, where the steel came too close to the surface in 1964 and has been rusting ever since. Those places have to be cut back to clean steel and filled. On a building like this, the filling is the part everyone sees.

Ciarán Nolan, the structural engineer on the project, and I had been through the 1964 steel drawings in the winter. They showed where the steel runs close to the surface, and they answered a question from our first visit: the shell is reinforced from end to end with a close mesh of bars, which is why no phone works underneath it. That part we are leaving as it is.

So before the scaffolding went up along the whole roof, it went up at one end, under the north fold, and I spent most of the spring in Dublin, on that scaffold, with small squares of concrete. I wrote the mixes. Gerry, the repair contractor's foreman, mixed and cast them, and has an opinion about every one.

## Why not a repair mortar from a bag

Repair mortars from a bag are strong, stick to almost anything, and are the colour of a new pavement. Put one on a sixty-two-year-old shell and it looks like a plaster on a knee.

They are also too strong, which surprised Ríona more than anything else I told her. A patch that is much harder and stiffer than the concrete around it doesn't move with it. The shell warms, cools and flexes; the patch doesn't, and a crack opens at its edge, exactly where you don't want water. The 1964 concrete isn't weak, but it is ordinary. Ciarán set the range: about as strong, not stronger.

Strength was the easy number. The hard part was what the patch looked like.

## What we were matching

Concrete's colour comes from the cement, which sets the grey, and the stones, which come through as the surface weathers. After six decades of Dublin rain, the underside of the Accordion is mostly stones. Look closely and it's speckled: grey-brown, a little rust, the odd white fleck, and every stone rounded. Rounded stones mean a river gravel, not crushed rock.

Samples 1 to 12, in April, tried the ordinary things: three cements, four sands, crushed limestone and crushed granite. The granite sparkled. The limestone was too even. All twelve looked like new concrete trying very hard.

Samples 13 to 26, in May, were river gravels from three suppliers. Closer, but too warm, like the roof seen through a cup of tea.

Then Callum, who reads everything, found the 1963 specification in a box in the bus company's basement. It named the gravel, and the quarry it came from, about forty kilometres up the river. The quarry is still working, a little further along the same bank. I rang them, and the man who answered said yes, they had supplied the bus station, and his grandfather had driven the lorry.

Samples 27 to 41 were all that gravel: different proportions, different sands, cured under wet hessian or plastic, cast against rough boards or smooth ones.

## How we judged them

Every sample went into a place on the north fold that needed cutting out anyway. Gerry gave each a chalk number, taped over, so nobody could favour their own.

Then we looked at them, by four rules:

- **From the floor, never from the scaffold.** Nobody waits for a bus on a scaffold.
- **Wet, dry, and the morning after rain.** Different stones darken at different speeds, and a patch that matches when dry can show up like a stain after a shower, which in Dublin is most of the time.
- **In Marta's light.** Marta photographs on grey days ([she wrote about why](/posts/photographing-concrete-in-flat-light)), because flat light has no shadows to flatter a texture. We pinned her prints side by side in the site cabin and argued in front of them.
- **Weeks old, not days.** Concrete has to cure and lose its skin before it shows you its stones.

That last rule is why there are forty-one. You can't hurry curing, so we made samples in batches and judged them weeks later. By the time 27 was old enough to judge, 28 to 41 were on the roof beside it.

## Twenty-seven

Twenty-seven was the plainest of the third batch: the old gravel, an ordinary grey cement, sand from the same quarry, cured under wet hessian, cast against rough boards. For its first week it looked wrong, greenish and too clean. Then it dried out, the first proper rain came, and on the morning after, standing on the terrazzo, I couldn't find it. Callum found it by counting boards from the end.

Several of the later samples beat it on one test or another. None beat it on all four.

## What I learned

- **Colour and aggregate first, strength second.** Strength is a number you can write down. Colour you have to go and look at, in bad weather, from where people stand.
- **Cover the numbers.** Everyone liked their own samples best until the tape went on.
- **Read the old specification before buying anything.** It would have saved us two batches and most of May.

The other forty patches come out again and are refilled with twenty-seven's mix, which Ciarán has signed off for the whole roof. Callum has all forty-one on his record. Twenty-seven stays where it is, the first piece of the repair, and already the hardest one to point out.
