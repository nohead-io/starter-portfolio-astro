A folded roof is a very good way to make thin concrete stand up, and a very good way to collect water. Every fold of the Accordion rises to a ridge and falls to a valley, and every drop of rain that lands on its 1,120 square metres ends up in one of fourteen valleys. From there it is meant to run to an outlet at the head of a column, down a cast-iron pipe hidden inside the column, and into a drain under the concourse floor.

When the Quays Bus Company asked us in, almost none of it was getting that far. [Callum's schedule](/posts/the-schedule-is-one-page) has one line for the roof: Repair. This is how we kept it dry.

## Where the water went

We started the way Callum starts everything, by looking before touching. In July, alongside the waiting room's doors, Jens drew every valley in section, one sheet each, with every crack and patch on it.

In 1964 the valleys were lined with strips of bitumen felt, which had long since gone brittle. Since then, someone had patched the worst places with asphalt, in at least four different decades, each a different colour. Water got under the patches, sat in the valleys, and found its way through the concrete at the cracks. Underneath, it arrived as drips, mostly over the platform 4 bay, and mostly on people.

But the real reason the valleys held water was the pipes.

## Fifty-eight years of pigeon

The downpipes run inside the columns, which means nobody can see when they are blocked. The bus company's maintenance binder records the pipes being rodded in March 1968. After that, nothing.

In August the drainage contractor put a camera down each pipe, with Jens and me watching the screen. Eleven of the fourteen were blocked by what the contractor called, professionally, "pigeon": nests, feathers, droppings, and the packed-down remains of all three, fifty-eight years deep. Out came a tennis ball, a comb, and a bus ticket from 1979, which Ríona has framed in her office.

Clearing them took four days of rods and water jets. Then every outlet got a mesh guard, so the pigeons can still use the roof, but not the pipes. They have moved up to the ridges, where they sit in a row and watch.

With the pipes clear, two of the leaks stopped on their own. They had never been leaks in the roof at all: water had backed up a blocked pipe and out through a joint into the column.

## Resealing the valleys

Then, one valley at a time, the repair contractor's crew, with Gerry, their foreman, stripped the asphalt and the old felt back to the 1964 concrete. Cracks and broken edges were repaired with the mix from [sample 27](/posts/forty-one-samples-of-concrete), the river gravel from the same quarry, so the repairs will weather to the same grey as the shell around them. Over that went a liquid membrane in two coats with a fleece between them, running a short way up each slope and finished at a straight line, so it reads as a stripe someone meant rather than a patch.

The slopes stay bare concrete, as Kavanagh & Rowe drew them. Water only stops long enough to do harm in the valleys.

## Testing before the rain did

We wanted to test the roof before Dublin did, in daylight, with someone standing underneath.

First Ciarán Nolan, our structural engineer, checked that the shell would carry it: a hand's depth of water in a valley weighs less than the snow the roof was designed for. Then the contractor plugged each outlet, filled the valley with water to the depth of a hand, and left it for forty-eight hours. Jens flew back for the test and stood underneath with a torch and his drawing of that valley, and marked anything damp in red. Twelve valleys stayed dry. Valley 6 was damp at an old construction joint; valley 11 had a hairline crack none of us had seen from above. Both were resealed, flooded again, and stayed dry.

Then we pulled the plugs and watched the water go. All fourteen valleys emptied in under ten minutes.

The first heavy rain after the test came on 9 September, a whole afternoon of it. Callum sent a photograph of a dry concourse floor, with no message.

## Why not the canopy

Before we were asked in, the plan was a steel canopy hung under the roof to catch the drips. It would have worked, in the sense that nobody waiting for a bus would have got wet.

But the water would still have been getting in. It would have soaked through the cracks to the steel inside the concrete, and rusting steel swells, breaking the concrete from the inside. All of it would have happened above the canopy, where nobody looks. A canopy would have turned a leak you could see into damage you couldn't.

It would also have hidden the roof, which was the one thing [everybody agreed to keep](/posts/what-do-we-keep).

## What failed

The concrete was fine. What failed was everything around it: the felt, the patches, the pipes, each with a shorter life than the roof and nobody whose job it was to look. The bus company's binder now has a new page, in Ríona's handwriting: every outlet, checked every October, after the leaves come down.
