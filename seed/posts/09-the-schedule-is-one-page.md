Every conservation job has a schedule of works: the document that says, part by part, what happens to the building. The longest one I've inherited ran to sixty pages, with a photograph for every item. The builders kept it in the site cabin, in its binder, on a shelf, with the plastic still on. When I asked the foreman which parts he used, he said, kindly, that he asked whoever was around.

So for the Accordion I made one rule: one page. Printed on one side, pinned in the site cabin at eye level, next to the kettle. If a line didn't fit, it wasn't a decision yet.

## Three words

Helena and I wrote about [how we decided what to keep](/posts/what-do-we-keep). This is how we told the builders. Every line on the page starts with one of three words:

- **Keep.** It stays as it is. Protect it while the work goes on around it. If it has to move for the works, it goes back exactly where it was.
- **Repair.** It stays, and someone works on it. How is written on the line.
- **Remove.** It goes. It is measured, photographed and recorded first.

That's all the vocabulary there is.

## How a line reads

Each line has the same five parts, in the same order: the word, the thing, where it is, what happens, and who to ask. This is the clock's, as it is on the wall:

> **Repair.** The clock over the departures board. Stopped at twenty past four since 1997. Take it down whole and send it to the clockmaker. Nothing on the case is cleaned on site. Ask Callum.

The rest read the same way. The terrazzo floor is a Keep: boards down before any scaffold goes up, nothing dragged across it, cracks recorded and not filled. The six timetable boards are a Keep too, numbered on the back, stored flat in the container and hung back on the same holes. The roof shell is a Repair, and its line says only to cut back to clean steel and fill with sample 27's mix. Sample 27 has [its own post](/posts/forty-one-samples-of-concrete). On the page it gets six words, because the page isn't where the reasons live.

## Why one page works

The reasons live in my record, which is long. Every line on the schedule has a page or three behind it: what the part is, when it was made, what we found, what we argued about and who won. The schedule can be short because the record isn't. Builders don't need the argument. They need the decision, and someone to ring.

When a decision changes, I reprint the page with the date and revision at the top, and the old one goes into the record. We're on revision seven.

And one page keeps me honest. A sixty-page schedule has room for "to be confirmed". This one doesn't. Every line has to be a decision before it can go on the wall.

## The line I lost

In revisions one and two, written while we were still arguing, the kiosk had a Keep line of its own, with my name on it.

The kiosk is not beautiful. It was put up seventeen years after the station opened, to sell newspapers and sweets, and it sold them for thirty years. Everyone wanted it gone: the bus company, the contractor, most of the studio. I argued that it was part of the building's life, that people still called it a meeting point, and that conservation isn't only for the parts we like.

I lost. In revision three the line became:

> **Remove.** The kiosk, 1981, against the south wall. Measured drawing and photographs first (Marta). Take down by hand, not by machine. Ask Callum.

Permission came on the first of July, and the kiosk came down the week after, by hand, as written. When the back panel came off the south wall, there was a stone behind it, set into the base of the wall, carved in plain capitals:

*This stone was laid on 4 May 1962*

Nobody knew it was there. Not the bus company, not the contractor, and not the Register: Declan's twenty-two years of pencil notes on card DMR 0412 don't mention it. Úna rang him that afternoon, and he was delighted to have something new to write on the back. The kiosk had been built against it in 1981, and for forty-five years the foundation stone of the Accordion had been behind the newspapers.

Revision four gave the stone a Keep line: clean it with water and a soft brush, and fix nothing within a metre of it. Revision five moved the Register's enamel plaque, taken down from the quay entrance for the works, to the south wall beside the waiting room's quay doors, where you read it and then see the stone through the glass.

## What I learned

I still think the kiosk should have stayed. I'm also glad it didn't. Both of those are in the record, which is where they belong.

The page is still one page. One line changed its first word, two lines were added, and the most important thing on the wall is something none of us wrote.
