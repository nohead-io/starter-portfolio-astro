People ask about the name more than anything else. Usually right after asking whether we are surveyors. So here is the answer, written down once, and with it the reason this journal exists.

## The drawing

A long section is what you get when you cut a building along its whole length, from the cellar to the roof, and draw the cut face. A plan shows you the rooms. An elevation shows you the outside. The long section shows how every part meets the next: where the stair lands, how the floor of one room becomes the ceiling of another, where the rain goes once it has hit the roof, and why that corridor is dark.

No client has ever asked to see one. They ask for plans, to count the rooms, and for views, to see what it will look like. The long section is the drawing architects make for themselves, and it is the one that tells you whether the building works.

## The rule

I spent ten years at a practice of two hundred people, drawing shopping centres and one hospital. My first boss there had one rule, printed and pinned above every desk in the building, all two hundred of them: *draw the long section first; if it doesn't work there, it doesn't work.*

I was twenty-four and thought it was the sort of thing older architects say to sound wise. Then I spent four months on a shopping centre that looked tidy in plan. In section, the delivery ramp met the underside of the cinema about a metre too early. Nobody had drawn the long section of a service yard. Somebody did eventually, at great expense, and after that I drew it first.

The hospital taught me the rest. A long section of a hospital is mostly ducts, and the ducts decide everything: the ceiling heights, the windows, whether a patient on the fourth floor can see a tree.

## The first drawing

I left in 2019 and started the studio in the first-floor room of a Georgian house in Dublin, with very tall windows and a good kettle. There were three of us: me, Callum, who looks after old buildings and keeps a record of every change made to them, and Elias, who built the bench on the half landing in our first month, so clients would have somewhere to wait.

We had desks and no clients. So we drew something for ourselves: the long section of the bus station on Fennell Quay, the one with the folded concrete roof that most of Dublin calls the Accordion. Callum found a set of Kavanagh & Rowe's original drawings in an archive, and we spent three weeks of evenings on it. In plan the roof is a pleated hat. In section it is what it really is: one thin sheet of concrete, folded fourteen times so it can carry itself, with the rain running down the valleys and into the columns.

It still hangs on the Dublin wall, and nobody may take it down. Clients waiting on the half landing can see it through the door. Most of them think it is a drawing of a radiator.

## Who we are now

Six of us, in two studios, and a dog.

- **Dublin**, where it started: me, Callum, and Aoife, our landscape architect, who will not let anyone draw a place until she has watched who uses it. Marta, who photographs our buildings, is based here when she isn't somewhere else.
- **Copenhagen**, since 2022, when Elias went home to open it: Elias, who leads design and makes the models, and Leela, who leads on concrete, water, heat and every detail where they meet. And Noir, Elias's pug. They are about to move into a new space, which Elias will write about once the racks are cleared.

## Why a journal

For seven years our work has mostly been explained in reports, to the people who paid for it. So when someone asks what we actually do, we describe it in the abstract, and "an architecture studio" covers ten thousand offices.

This journal is where we show the work instead: what we did, what went wrong, what we would do again. A few rules for ourselves, borrowed in spirit from that pinned sheet of paper:

1. **Say what happened.** Things that happened, and what we made of them.
2. **Name the people.** Every post is written by someone, and the work it describes was done by someone.
3. **Show the mistakes.** A post about a project that went perfectly would be fiction, and a dull one.

This year, that is mostly going to be one building. Last autumn the Quays Bus Company rang to ask whether we would look at a leaking roof on Fennell Quay. Seven years after we drew it for nothing, someone has asked us to look at it properly. We spent the winter getting to know it, and the work starts in April. More on that soon.

> Draw the long section first. If it doesn't work there, it doesn't work.

If you come to the studio, ask to see the long section. Nobody ever does.
