I buy index cards the way other people buy milk. The stationer near the studio keeps a box behind the counter for me, and has stopped asking what they are for.

Helena's rule is the long section first; she wrote about it [a few weeks ago](/posts/why-were-called-long-section). Mine comes before hers. Before anyone draws a place, somebody should watch it: who comes, where they go, where they stop, which way they face, and how long they stay. Draw the people first. The building can wait a week.

## Why watch first

Every drawing of a place is a set of guesses about what people will do there. Sit here, walk this way, wait under that. Architects are good at guessing and very confident about it, and the guesses are wrong in ways that are expensive to fix in concrete and cheap to fix on paper.

Watching is not research in any grand sense. It is sitting somewhere unremarkable for a morning, then an evening, then a wet afternoon, and writing down what happens. Most of it is dull. The dull parts are where the drawing goes wrong.

## One observation per card

The method fits on the back of one card:

- **One observation per card.** What happened, where, when, and for how long. "07:52. Man with a dog crosses the grass on the diagonal, not on the path. Third today."
- **No opinions on the card.** "People prefer the diagonal" is an opinion. Three men with dogs is a fact. The opinion comes later, at the table.
- **Every kind of weather.** Watch in the weather the place mostly has, not the weather you'd like.

Why cards and not a notebook? Because a notebook keeps observations in the order you saw them, which is the least interesting order there is. Cards can be dealt out on a table and sorted into piles: everyone who stopped, everyone who sat, everyone who turned round. The piles are the first drawing. Some piles are much taller than you expect, and one or two are empty, and the empty ones are often the most useful.

## The Coast Road

This started properly on the Coast Road last year: nine timber shelters at the viewpoints of a 180-kilometre road around two bays in the west. Our first sketches did what everyone does at a viewpoint. A bench facing the view, a roof opening towards it, the sea framed like a postcard.

Then I spent four days at the viewpoints, in March, over two visits, with a flask and two hundred cards. People got out of their cars, walked to the edge, and turned round. Not away from the view entirely, but sideways to it, shoulders up, backs to the wind coming in off the water. Parents stood between their children and the weather. A couple shared a sandwich with their backs to the sea and looked at the view over their shoulders. On the second visit I stopped writing "stood with back to the wind" and just drew an arrow.

By the end there were two hundred and thirty cards, and the tallest pile was the one with the arrows. The shelters were turned round. Each one has its roof facing away from the weather, a bench in its lee, and the view framed by a single opening in the side, so you look at the sea from shelter instead of into the wind. A boatyard in the second bay built all nine in larch.

The first one went in on a grey Thursday. The next morning a woman sat on its bench with a flask of her own and stayed for forty minutes. I have the card.

## What I got wrong

The first cards from the Coast Road were full of opinions. "People like the north end." I had to throw away a day of them. I also watched the first viewpoint only in sunshine, because that was the week we had, and it was nearly the wrong week to have: the sun brought people who don't come the rest of the year. The second visit, in the rain, gave me the arrows.

And I watched only the people who stopped. The ones who slowed down, looked, and drove on are a pile too. It turned out to be the tallest one at the windiest viewpoint, which is how we knew that shelter had to be longer.

## The concourse

This year the studio has a bus station to look at: a concourse on Fennell Quay under a folded concrete roof, open to the river, where people wait for buses in all weathers. Part of the brief is somewhere warm to wait, and the forecourt on the quay outside, where people wait too, is mine to redraw.

Before anyone draws a warm room, I am going to watch the concourse for a week, at every hour the buses run: where people stand, how long they wait, which way they face, and where nobody sits. There will be a stopwatch this time, and a lot more cards. The stationer has already ordered a second box.
