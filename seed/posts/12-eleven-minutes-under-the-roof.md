Before we draw anything new in an old building, we watch it. Aoife has [written about why](/posts/draw-the-people-before-the-building): the people who use a place already know what it's for, and they show you if you stand still long enough.

In the spring Aoife had watched the Accordion's concourse on and off, and drawn what she saw. In July we did it properly. For one week, Monday the sixth to Sunday the twelfth, the two of us stood on the concourse and timed everyone who waited for a bus. It was Jens's first full week at Long Section. He has [described the rest of that month](/posts/my-first-month-mostly-in-dublin).

## How we counted

We watched from seven to ten in the morning and from four to eight in the evening, every day of the week, Sunday included. Aoife took the mornings and Jens the evenings, and on the busiest evenings we were both there.

We counted only people who waited. Anyone walking straight through to a bus didn't count; anyone who stood still for more than a minute did. Each of them got an index card. Aoife always has index cards; it turned out she had brought four packs.

Every card had the same six things on it:

- the time they started waiting, and the time they left
- where they stood, by fold, counted from the north end
- standing or sitting, and on what
- alone or with someone
- what they carried
- which way they faced

We used cards and not our phones for the simplest reason: under the roof a phone can't find a signal, and a stopwatch and a pencil don't need one. A card also doesn't look like a survey. Two people asked us what we were doing. When we said "timing you", one of them asked how long she'd been waiting. Fourteen minutes. She didn't look surprised.

By Sunday night we had 312 cards.

## What the cards said

We spread them out on the long table in the Dublin studio, under the long section, and sorted them three ways before we believed any of it.

**The average wait was eleven minutes.** Some waits were a minute or two; the longest was fifty-two, a man who missed one bus and waited for the next rather than walk. Most were between six and sixteen. Eleven minutes is a particular length of time. It's long enough to get cold, and too short to go anywhere else.

**41% of people stood under the deepest fold.** That's 128 cards out of 312, all in one stretch of a concourse with fourteen folds. The roof comes lowest there, and the wind off the quay goes over it instead of through it. It's also where you can see the departures board and the clock above it, and with no signal, the board is the only thing that tells you when your bus is coming.

**Nobody sat on the old benches.** Not one person, all week. The benches run along the quay side, facing the bays, which made sense in 1964, when you watched for your bus. They also face straight into the wind. People sat on their suitcases, on the ledge under the timetable boards, on the bottom step by the south door, and once, on a Saturday morning, on a folded newspaper on the terrazzo. They did not sit on the benches.

## What it changed

The planning drawings and the model had already put the glass waiting room under the deepest fold; Elias tells that story in [his post about it](/posts/a-model-you-can-lift-the-roof-off). The week turned a good guess into a drawing with dimensions. The most people we counted under that fold at one time was thirty-one, on the Friday evening, so the room holds about thirty: twenty-four sitting and the rest standing near the doors. People stay eleven minutes, so the room is heated. Most of them wait in the evening, so it's lit, and from the quay you can see the roof glow above it.

The cards turned the benches round. The new oak benches in the waiting room face into the room and towards the departures board, with their backs to the quay. Elias redrew them the week the cards were sorted, and Birthe checked the joints in the joinery before any were made.

The cards went outside too. A few came from the forecourt, the strip of quay between the concourse and the river wall, which is Aoife's to redraw: smokers, and people watching for their bus along the quay, every one of them with their back to the river. So the forecourt gets a low granite wall to lean or sit on, facing the concourse, granite paving laid along the line of the folds, and four alders between the wall and the water, to take the edge off the wind once they've grown.

## What the week showed us

- **Count only the people who wait.** The people passing through are a different building.
- **Watch a Sunday.** Sunday's waiters were older, slower and more often in pairs, and they stood under the deepest fold more than anyone.
- **Write down which way people face.** It was the last line we added to the card, and it's the one that moved the benches.
- **Sort the cards more than once.** The first sort, by time, told us almost nothing. The second, by place, told us almost everything.

The 312 cards are in a shoebox on Aoife's desk. Aoife has bought more.
