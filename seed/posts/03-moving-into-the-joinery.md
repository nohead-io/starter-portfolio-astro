For four years the Copenhagen half of Long Section worked from two desks in a shared office by the canal. It was fine. The coffee was free and the radiators had opinions. The trouble was the models: there was nowhere to cut anything, so I made them on my kitchen table at night and carried them in on the bus, on my knees, and apologised to whoever sat next to me.

In January, a friend sent me a listing with no photographs and one line of description: *former joinery, ground floor, some machinery remains.* I went to see it the next morning.

## What remained

The joinery made doors, windows, stairs and, for a few years in the eighties, church pews. It closed in 2019, when the owner, Birthe, retired after running it for thirty-four years. Her father ran it before that. The space had been empty since, and "some machinery" turned out to mean:

- Eleven timber racks along the back wall, floor to ceiling. Nine were empty. Two still hold oak boards, stacked with thin sticks between them so the air can move, seasoning since 2017.
- A panel saw the size of a small car, which I am not allowed to touch.
- A cupboard of sample boards: oak, ash, elm, larch and a dozen I couldn't name, each labelled in pencil with where it came from and the year.
- A band saw in the back room, older than me and better maintained.

Birthe still lives two streets away. She came to the viewing with a thermos of coffee, told us which rack sticks, and said the oak was not for sale. When I asked when it would be, she said it would be ready when it was ready.

## The racks

A timber rack looks like shelving and isn't. The longest boards lie lowest, so they stay flat. The wood needed soonest is at the front. Every board has air on both sides, and the sticks between them are spaced so nothing bends while it dries. Someone looked at how the wood was used and how it behaves, and built the rack around both.

We kept all eleven. Seven now hold rolled drawings, books and the boxes of material samples every architect owns and nobody can explain. Two are kept empty for models, so a model in progress has somewhere to sit where nobody will put a coffee cup on it. Two still hold Birthe's oak, and we dust round it.

## The panel saw

The panel saw stays where it is, because moving it would mean taking down a wall. Nobody may touch it. This is Birthe's rule, and it is also the insurer's, and I have never seen two parties agree so quickly about anything.

## The band saw

The band saw is the reason the joinery made sense. The back room is now where the models are made: card, balsa, limewood, at 1:100 for the early ideas and 1:50 once we know what we are doing.

Birthe offered to teach us to use it properly, and now she comes in on Friday afternoons with a thermos and a tin of biscuits. So far she has taught Leela and me to set the fence, change a blade without swearing, and cut a curve in one pass instead of six. She calls every model "the doll's house", whatever it is. Our first was a test: a long box with a folded lid. She looked at it for a while, said "a nice doll's house", and showed me where the lid would split.

The next one will be harder. The bus station Helena is about to write about has a roof of fourteen folds, and someone will want to lift it off. [More on that later](/posts/a-model-you-can-lift-the-roof-off).

## Noir

On the day we moved in, my pug, Noir, walked straight past the desks and the kitchen into the back room, lay down under the workbench in the sawdust, and refused to come out. We gave up and brought her blanket. It lives there now, and so does she.

She gets Birthe's biscuits first, before anyone else, and Birthe pretends this isn't happening.

## The studio now

The front room, with the windows onto the street, is where we work: four desks, the cupboard of sample boards, and a long table that seats twelve, which is where the workshops will happen. The back room belongs to the band saw, the workbench, the models and Noir, roughly in that order. There is a kitchen with a stove that predates all of us.

> Birthe says a joinery is a room arranged around the wood. We are trying to arrange a studio around the drawings and the models. We are not there yet.

Two neighbours have already come by on a Friday afternoon. Birthe let each of them cut something on the band saw. Both pieces were crooked. Nobody touched the panel saw.
