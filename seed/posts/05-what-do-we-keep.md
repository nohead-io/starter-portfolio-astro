Every repair starts with a list of what stays. It is easy to write a list with everything on it, and easy to write one with only the roof. The work is in the middle, and the middle took us three weeks, two site visits and one argument we are still a little sore about.

This is how we decided, for the bus station on Fennell Quay that [Helena wrote about](/posts/a-bus-station-with-six-names) at the start of the month.

## How we decide

Callum has four questions, and he asks them of everything, down to the door handles:

1. **Is it original, or part of the building's life since?** Both count. Neither wins automatically.
2. **Does it still do its job, or could it again?**
3. **Would anyone miss it?** Not us. The people who use the building.
4. **Does it stop the building doing what it is for now?**

"Is it beautiful?" is not one of the questions. Plenty of beautiful things have to go, and plenty of plain ones are the reason a building works.

## The roof

The easy one, and the reason for the brief. A steel canopy underneath would have kept the roof and hidden it, which is a slower way of losing it. Keeping the roof means repairing the concrete as concrete: finding a mix that matches what Kavanagh & Rowe poured in 1964, and resealing every valley so the water goes where they meant it to. Leela has started on the samples, and [will write about them](/posts/forty-one-samples-of-concrete) once they have cured.

## The terrazzo

The concourse floor is grey and white terrazzo, cracked in places and stained by sixty years of wet shoes. It stays as it is: the cracks are recorded, not filled.

What decided it was something nobody had noticed. The floor is divided by thin brass strips, and when Callum measured them, they line up with the valleys of the roof above. The floor repeats the roof, fold for fold. Declan, at the Register, said he had walked across it for forty years and never seen it, and then added a note to the card.

## The clock

The clock over the departures board says twenty past four, and has since 1997. One suggestion was to keep it stopped, as a sort of monument. We decided against that. A stopped clock in a bus station is a joke at the passengers' expense, and they have had it for twenty-nine years. It stays, and we will try to get it going. Whether it can go again is a question for a clockmaker, and we have found one.

## The timetable boards

The painted timetable boards no longer show the timetable; screens do that now. They stay anyway. Under a roof with no phone signal, people look up to find out where they are going, and the boards are where they look. What goes on them can be decided later. Taking them down can't be undone.

## The kiosk

The kiosk was added in 1981: brick, glass and a striped awning, against the south wall of the concourse. It sold papers and sweets until its shutters came down in 2011. Everyone wanted it gone. Ríona wanted it gone. The drivers wanted it gone. Declan's card says it blocks the view to the quay, and adds "shame".

Callum argued for it, properly, for a week. His case: the kiosk is part of the building's life, and forty-five years is not nothing. Thirty of those years people bought their papers there on the way to work. Conservation is not choosing a building's best year and deleting all the others. If every addition gets removed because the original was better, the building ends up as a drawing of itself.

Helena's case was about where it stands. The kiosk sits under the deepest fold of the roof, which is the most sheltered place on the concourse and the one spot out of the wind off the quay. Whatever goes there, it shouldn't be a closed kiosk.

Both of those things are true. We went back on a windy Thursday and stood where the kiosk stands, and then where everything else stands, and the shelter won. The kiosk goes, if the council agrees. The station is a protected structure, so taking anything away needs permission, and so does adding a room. Both went into the planning application at the start of the month, with Callum's report on what we keep and why, and the waiting room drawn where the kiosk stands. If permission comes in time, the kiosk comes down in July. Elias's model will tell us whether the room is in the right place before the council does.

Callum took losing the way he takes most things: by writing it down. The kiosk has been measured and drawn, Marta is photographing every face of it, and the sign over the hatch goes into the bus company's store, labelled in Callum's hand.

## Writing it down

All of this, every element of the building from the roof to the door handles, with keep, repair or remove and the reason, goes on a single page. Callum started it in April, and it is on its third revision. It is the page everyone on the project works from, contractor included, and [he will write about it](/posts/the-schedule-is-one-page) once the builders have used it.

We have agreed not to reopen the argument about the kiosk. Callum has agreed to this twice, which tells you something.
