Fennell Quay Bus Station reopened on Saturday 10 October, the first night of Open House weekend, at half past seven in the evening, in its own concourse. A hundred and twenty people came: bus drivers, the Quays Bus Company's board, people from the city council, Úna and Frances from the Modern Register, Declan in his good coat, and a brass quartet who played under the folded roof and sounded, everyone agreed, enormous.

The buses kept running the whole time. A reopening that closed the station for a night seemed to miss the point.

## Half past seven

At 7:30, Ríona Kelly climbed a stepladder in front of the departures board and set the clock going. It had stopped at twenty past four one afternoon in 1997, and nobody had been able to say which afternoon. A clockmaker from the north side had spent August with its works on his bench. When the second hand moved, the concourse applauded a clock, which I had not seen before.

Then Declan went into the new waiting room and pulled a dust sheet off the foundation stone on its back wall, the one that had been [behind the kiosk](/posts/the-schedule-is-one-page) for forty-five years. *This stone was laid on 4 May 1962.* He read it out, though everyone could see it.

The quartet played. The roof did what Kavanagh & Rowe must have hoped it would and held the sound in its folds. Under the deepest fold, the new waiting room glowed, with its oak benches and its heaters on, exactly as Elias had drawn it.

At 7:52, I noticed that people in the waiting room were wearing their coats.

## What happened

The waiting room has two pairs of glass doors, one facing the concourse and one facing the quay. Jens drew them in July, light enough to push open with an elbow while carrying a bag. We then set them to swing open on their own, on sensors, because it seemed kinder: nobody would have to push anything with a suitcase, a buggy or a wheelchair.

What we had never done was stand there on a windy night.

On Saturday the wind came straight off the river, along the quay and in through the open side of the concourse. The sensors saw everything it carried: a programme, the edge of someone's coat, the quartet's music on its stand, leaves from the quay. Every gust looked like a person arriving. The quay doors opened, stayed open, and the concourse doors opened too, because people were walking towards them to find out why it was cold. By the time I noticed, the waiting room's heaters had been heating Fennell Quay for twenty minutes.

We had been warned, by [Aoife and Jens's own count](/posts/eleven-minutes-under-the-roof). In the observation week, 41% of the people waiting stood under the deepest fold, out of the wind, and nobody sat on the old benches because they faced it. We knew where the wind came from. We put a room there to keep people out of it, and then gave the room doors that opened whenever the wind asked.

## Twenty minutes

The fix was simpler than the problem. The doors have a switch inside the operator housing, above the frame, that changes them from the sensors to a push button. The contractor's electrician, who had come to the party as a guest and was holding a glass of wine, found a ladder, borrowed my torch, and switched both pairs. By 8:14 the doors were closed, and staying closed.

By nine the waiting room was full, every seat taken, which is the first time I have seen anyone choose to sit down in that building.

## What we changed

Nothing, beyond what the electrician did that night. The doors still open on their own, for anyone who presses the steel button beside them, at hand height on both sides, and they close themselves gently behind you. The wind can't press a button. I have been back twice and watched people with suitcases, buggies and a wheelchair go in and out without stopping.

In the three days since, nobody has asked for the sensors back.

## What I learned

The mistake was mine. Handover was my job; the door settings were on my list, and I signed them off on a still afternoon in September, with the doors opening and closing beautifully for me and nobody else.

What I should have asked was the question we ask about everything else: when will this be used, and what will the weather be doing? We had flooded every valley of [the roof](/posts/fourteen-valleys-and-a-lot-of-pigeons) before trusting the rain to test it. We didn't do the same for wind and a pair of doors, because doors are ordinary, and ordinary things don't get tested. They should. It would have taken one evening on the quay with the doors switched on and a few people walking past.

I have added it to my own list for every handover: test it on the worst night, not the best afternoon.

The quartet stayed until eleven. Declan waited for the last bus in the waiting room, sitting down, with his coat open, and wrote something on a small card he keeps in his pocket. He wouldn't tell me what.
