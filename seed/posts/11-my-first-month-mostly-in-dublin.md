I joined Long Section on the first of July. On the second of July, Elias handed me a roll of drawings, a site pass and a plane ticket.

"The waiting room needs someone on site," he said. "And you'll learn faster there."

He was right on both counts. This is what I learned.

## A studio of two halves

I had only seen the Dublin studio on the Monday video call, where it looks like a bookshelf with Callum in front of it. In person it's the first floor of a Georgian house, with very tall windows, and on the long wall [the long section of the Accordion's roof](/posts/why-were-called-long-section), the first drawing the studio made together. Nobody is allowed to take it down. On my first morning I was told this twice, by two different people, before I had taken my coat off.

Clients wait on the half landing, on a bench Elias built. I sat on it for ten minutes on the first day because I didn't know which door was the studio. It's a good bench.

The two studios work differently. Copenhagen is quiet and deep: long stretches of drawing, the band saw on Fridays, and a pug asleep under the workbench. Dublin is louder. People pin things up to argue in front of them, and nothing leaves the studio until someone else has read it.

## No signal under the roof

The Accordion is a twenty-minute walk from the studio, along the river to Fennell Quay. I went most days.

The first thing you learn on site is that your phone stops working the moment you step under the roof. Fourteen folds of concrete shell, and not one bar. On my first visit I stood in the middle of the concourse trying to open a drawing and watched the little wheel turn until I gave up and walked out onto the quay, where it opened at once and started raining on it.

After that I printed everything. A3, folded into my jacket, the day's drawings and a pencil. By the second week the people on site had started borrowing my prints, because theirs were on their phones.

I was there in the second week of July when the 1981 kiosk came down, by hand, and the foundation stone was found behind it. Callum has [written about that](/posts/the-schedule-is-one-page), better than I can. All I'll add is that he stood looking at the stone for a long time without saying anything, which I've since learned is unusual.

In my first full week I also spent the evenings on the concourse timing people waiting for buses, on index cards, while Aoife took the mornings. That week deserves a post of its own.

## The doors

My job was the waiting room: the glass room Elias designed under the deepest fold, [where the model put it](/posts/a-model-you-can-lift-the-roof-off). More exactly, its doors. A glass room is mostly walls you can see through, and the doors are the only part of it people actually touch, so they have to be right.

I drew two pairs, one facing the concourse and one facing the quay, in oak frames to match the benches, with glass that goes almost to the floor. I drew them eleven times. Elias sent back the first six from Copenhagen with one word each. Leela sent back the seventh with a page about thresholds and drainage. Birthe, through Elias, had views on the joints.

The wind comes straight off the river and through the concourse, and I worried about it. I spent one afternoon standing where the doors would go, getting cold, watching which way the newspapers blew. It was a useful afternoon. I can tell you a great deal about where newspapers go on Fennell Quay.

## Things I learned in Dublin

- A **quay** is a street along the river, pronounced "key". I said it the other way for a week. Everyone was very kind about it.
- Buses on the quay come when they feel like it. On the Accordion's concourse, people look at the departures board instead of their phones, because their phones can't help them.
- The kettle in the studio is on before anyone says good morning. I now do this in Copenhagen as well, and Leela has noticed.
- Irish rain is not like Danish rain. It's softer, and it never quite stops.

I'm back in Copenhagen now. Noir has decided to tolerate me, which Elias says is a big step. The doors are on the contractor's drawings, the oak for their frames is cut, and my raincoat is still drying on the back of the joinery door.
