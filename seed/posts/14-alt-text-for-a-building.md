When the photographs of the Accordion started to pile up, Callum asked who would write the alt text: the short description of each image that a screen reader reads aloud, and that appears when an image doesn't load. There are 1,400 photographs so far, and about two hundred of them are on our own site, in this journal and on the project's page.

I said I would. I took the photographs, so I know what each one is for. And I had a feeling that if it went to someone else in a spreadsheet, it would come back as the same brutalist building two hundred times.

Each photograph is uploaded to the site once, with its description beside it, and the description goes wherever the photograph goes: a post, the project's gallery, a link preview. So it has to make sense on its own, wherever someone meets it.

## What a description is for

A photograph of a building does a job. It shows you the roof from the quay, or the way in under the lowest fold, or the patch of concrete that was sample 27. The description should do the same job, in words, for someone who can't see it.

So the question for every photograph isn't what it is, but why it's here, and what you would miss if it were gone.

## Four questions

For each photograph, I ask:

1. **What does it show that the words around it don't already say?** The building's name is usually the heading. Saying it again means a screen reader says it twice. The photograph is there for something else.
2. **What would you notice first, standing there?** The roof, the shape, the material, the size of it against a person.
3. **What is it like?** One plain comparison, if it helps. Not two.
4. **Is it a style, or a description?** Brutalist is a style. It tells you what a building is called in a book. Raw concrete, with the marks of the boards it was cast against, tells you what it looks like.

## A roof in one sentence

The hardest photograph to describe was the first one anyone sees: the whole building from across the river. My first draft was:

> Fennell Quay Bus Station, a brutalist building from 1964.

Everything in that sentence is already on the page, apart from the style, which explains nothing. The second draft went too far the other way, a folded-plate thin-shell roof of fourteen bays on slender columns: accurate, and you would need to be an engineer to see it.

The version that stayed:

> A long, low building under a concrete roof folded into deep zigzags, like a sheet of paper creased and opened out.

One comparison, and it's the one the city already uses: the building's nicknames are mostly about paper and music. I didn't count the folds. You can only see nine of them from the river, and a sentence that says fourteen describes the building, not the photograph.

## As it is

That turned out to be the rule I broke most often: describing what I know instead of what the photograph shows.

I know that the square on the north fold is sample 27, and that it matched. The photograph doesn't know that. It shows rows of small square patches on the underside of the roof, each numbered in chalk, one of them nearly invisible against the concrete around it. The [post about the samples](/posts/forty-one-samples-of-concrete) can tell you which one; the description tells you what you would have seen.

The same with the valleys. I photographed them during [the waterproofing](/posts/fourteen-valleys-and-a-lot-of-pigeons), and my first note called one the leaking valley. There is no water in the photograph, only a narrow gully of grey concrete between two sloping planes, with a coiled hose at the far end, so that is what the description says.

## What I leave out

Colour, almost always, because it rarely tells you what a photograph is for, and in [flat light](/posts/photographing-concrete-in-flat-light) there is little of it. Weather, unless it matters. People, unless they show the scale: a man with a suitcase walking underneath tells you how high the roof is, and nothing else would.

And I never write "image of" or "photo of". A screen reader already says it's an image.

## Where to cut

Writing a description is a lot like drawing a section. You decide where to cut, and everything on either side of the line is left out on purpose. A good one is short because of what you chose, not because you ran out of things to say.

Callum now reads every description before it goes on the site, the same way he reads every drawing. He has changed four words in two hundred. One was zigzags, which he wanted to be folds. It is still zigzags.
