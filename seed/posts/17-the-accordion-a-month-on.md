In March I wrote about [why we're called Long Section](/posts/why-were-called-long-section), and about the first drawing we made together: the long section through the Accordion's folded roof, which still hangs on the wall in Dublin. We drew it for ourselves, before we had a client. Seven years later it helped answer a real question: whether a roof that leaked could be kept. It has been a month since the building reopened. This is a look at how it is being used, which was always the part we cared about most.

## Open House weekend

Over the two days of Open House, 3,100 people came through the doors.

Callum gave tours every hour on the Sunday, starting at the quay entrance and ending in the waiting room, at the foundation stone, and ran out of voice by three. People asked him the same two questions: why the roof was folded, and where the kiosk had gone. He answered the first with a sheet of paper, creased into a zigzag and stood on its edge. He answered the second very fairly, for someone who [argued to keep it](/posts/what-do-we-keep).

## People sit down

In the observation week, Aoife and Jens timed 312 people waiting on the concourse. Nobody, in a whole week, sat on the old benches, which faced the wind. That was the brief, in the end: [give people somewhere warm to wait](/posts/a-bus-station-with-six-names).

On the last Tuesday in October, in the rain, Aoife went back and counted for an hour at six o'clock. Forty-three people waited for a bus. Thirty-one of them sat down over the hour, in the waiting room, on the oak benches Birthe had checked joint by joint. Two of them were asleep. One was reading a hardback with her shoes off. The doors open at the press of a button, and in a month nobody has asked for the sensors back.

From the quay in the evening, the waiting room is lit and the underside of the roof glows above it. Marta photographed it last week, from across the river, and described it as "a lantern under a folded sheet". I let her have the comparison.

## The clock

The clock over the departures board has been going since 7:30 on reopening night. It loses about four seconds a week. A bus driver tells Ríona exactly how many every Monday, and the clockmaker comes once a month to put it right. Several people have told the bus company that it is the first clock in Dublin they have set their watches by since 1997.

## Ríona's view

I asked Ríona Kelly, the Quays Bus Company's head of estates, what she would say a month on. She thought for a while and said this:

> I asked for another opinion because the canopy frightened me, and I couldn't say why. The answer I got was to spend a week watching people wait for buses. I thought that was a strange thing to pay for. It was the most useful week of the project. The complaints we get now are about the buses.

## DMR 0412

The Modern Register's black enamel plaque went back on the building on 19 October, moved from the quay entrance to the south wall beside the waiting room's quay doors, so you read the plaque and then see the stone through the glass.

Úna brought card DMR 0412 over that afternoon to show us. On the front, unchanged: the building's name, its address, 1964, Kavanagh & Rowe. On the back, under twenty-two years of Declan Moran's pencil, there is one new line, copied from the card he was writing on in the waiting room on [reopening night](/posts/reopening-night):

> 10.x.26. Clock going. Stone visible. Waited for the last bus sitting down, for the first time. Warm. D.M.

Frances Rowe, who started the Register in 1989 and corrects everyone by postcard, has not sent one. Úna says that is the highest praise the Register gives.

## What the year taught us

Look before you draw. Every good decision on the Accordion came from somebody watching first: Callum reading the records, Aoife timing the concourse, Leela [flooding the valleys](/posts/fourteen-valleys-and-a-lot-of-pigeons) before the rain could. The bad one came from not watching on the right night.

Repair before you add. The canopy would have been the new thing. Clearing fifty-eight years of pigeon out of the pipes was the right one.

And draw the long section first. The roof only made sense cut along its whole length: the folds, the valleys, the pipes in the columns, the drain under the floor, and the people standing underneath, in the one place out of the wind.

## What's next

In December we are entering a competition for a small library in a market town: a reading room and a square in front of it, where the market has been held on Thursdays for longer than anyone has recorded. Elias has started a model. Birthe has asked whether it is another doll's house. It is.

And a maritime museum has asked us to look at a boathouse on its slipway, as a home for its collection of ten thousand ship plans, which currently live in a room with no windows. Leela's first question was how high the tide comes. Callum's was whether anyone had drawn the boathouse's long section. Nobody had. He's started.
