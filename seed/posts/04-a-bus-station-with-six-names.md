I have caught buses from Fennell Quay since I was a student. Seven years ago we spent three weeks drawing [its long section](/posts/why-were-called-long-section) from a set of old drawings. In all that time I had never stood under its roof with a tape measure and a reason to be there. Last October, I did.

## One more opinion

Ríona Kelly is head of estates at the Quays Bus Company, which runs the station. She met Callum and me at the timetable boards on a wet Tuesday and walked us round without an umbrella, which told us how long she has worked there.

The roof leaks. It has leaked for years, mostly along the valleys between the folds, and on a wet day there are buckets on the concourse under three of them. The company had a plan, priced and nearly approved: a steel canopy hung under the concrete, catching the water before it reached anyone. It would work, more or less. It would also hide the roof from underneath, for good.

Ríona wanted one more opinion before she signed it. "I'd like to be sure," she said. "Once it's up, nobody's going to take it down."

## The building

The station was built in 1964, by Kavanagh & Rowe, on the quay by the river. Its roof is fourteen folds of thin concrete shell, 1,120 square metres of it, resting on columns that carry the rainwater down inside them. Underneath is the concourse, open to the quay on one side: a terrazzo floor, the old timetable boards, a clock over the departures board, and against the south wall, a kiosk from 1981 with its shutters down.

The clock says twenty past four. Ríona says it has said twenty past four since 1997.

## Six names

Nobody calls it Fennell Quay Bus Station except the signs. In one morning we counted six names:

- **The Accordion**, which is what most of Dublin calls it, and what we call it.
- **The Concertina**, from the older passengers, who will tell you there is a difference.
- **The Paper Fan**, from a class of schoolchildren whose drawings of it are pinned in Ríona's office.
- **The Crinkle**, from the drivers, on the radio.
- **Kavanagh's Folly**, from the architects who didn't like it in 1964, and a few who still don't.
- **"The bus station"**, which is what everyone says when they are late.

A building that people have bothered to give six names to is loved by more of them than its buckets suggest.

## No signal

Under the roof, there is no phone signal at all. Mine gave up a step inside the concourse and came back a step outside. Ríona said everyone notices and nobody knows why. Leela, on the phone from Copenhagen that evening, said she had a few ideas and would like to see the steel drawings first.

It also means nobody waiting can check their phone for the bus. They look up at the boards, or out at the quay. I wrote that down, and Aoife, who will [watch the concourse](/posts/draw-the-people-before-the-building) before anyone draws on it, wrote it down too.

## Card DMR 0412

Callum starts every old building in its records, and the first place he looked for this one was a drawer.

The Modern Register is a small Dublin charity that records the city's twentieth-century buildings on 1,912 index cards, in six oak drawers. Úna Fitzgerald, the registrar, opened the third drawer and took out DMR 0412. The front is typed: the building's name, its address, the year and the architects. The back is pencil, twenty-two years of it, in the small, square hand of Declan Moran, a retired architect who volunteers there. Some of it:

- *Valley 9 leaking. Bucket.*
- *Kiosk (1981) blocks the view to the quay. Shame.*
- *Pigeons in the downpipes? Water comes out of the column base, not the drain.*

Úna lent us the card, which she has never done before, and the Register's photographs of the building going back to the sixties. Callum read the card before he read anything else, and Declan's note about the pigeons sent him looking down the columns before he looked up at the roof.

The Register's black enamel plaque is on the wall by the quay entrance. Declan straightened it while we were there.

## The brief

Back in the studio, I wrote the brief on three lines and pinned it under the long section:

1. Stop the leaks.
2. Keep the roof.
3. Give people somewhere warm to wait.

Ríona read it, put the canopy back in its folder, and asked when we could start. We spent the winter on the survey, and in March we went back with the brief agreed and the scaffold booked for April. The first job is deciding what else to keep, and that is the next post.
