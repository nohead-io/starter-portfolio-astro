Most architectural photographers wait for sun. Hard light, deep shadows, a blue sky behind the building. It makes a striking picture, and for a repair it is the wrong picture.

Sun on concrete hides the concrete. The bright faces burn out and the shadows go black, and what disappears in between is everything a repair needs to see: the board marks left by the 1964 formwork, the size and colour of the aggregate, the patch where someone mended a fold in the seventies and it doesn't quite match. On a flat, grey morning, the whole surface is readable. So I spent three weeks, from the end of May, waiting for the weather everyone in Dublin complains about. It was not a long wait.

## The roof in flat light

The bus station on Fennell Quay is impossible in sun. Its roof is fourteen folds of thin concrete, and in direct light half of them go white and half go black, and the whole thing looks like a cardboard model of itself.

On the third morning it was overcast and still, and the roof turned into what Helena's [long section](/posts/why-were-called-long-section) says it is: a single sheet of concrete, folded like paper. Flat light also shows the water. You can follow every leak from the outside, as a dark stain running down a valley, and from the inside, as a tide mark around the top of a column. I photographed every valley from both sides, and Callum now has a set of fourteen pairs pinned up in the Dublin studio, which he calls the evidence.

## Where people stand

The brief for every photograph was simple: stand where a person stands.

No drones, no ladders, no wide-angle lens pressed against the far wall. The building was photographed at eye height, from the places where people actually are: at the bus bays, on the quay, at the timetable boards, by the door they come in through, and under the deepest fold, where the kiosk still stands until July.

It makes the photographs plainer than an architecture magazine's. It also makes them honest about the building people use. A repair is for the people waiting for the 6:40, and the photographs should show the roof the way they see it, from underneath, slightly craned.

## Before, during, after

I marked thirty spots on a copy of the concourse plan and numbered them. So far I have photographed the building from all thirty, at the same height, with the same lens, about four hundred photographs in all. That is the before.

As the works go on, I'll go back to the same spots, and again when they're finished. A photograph taken from the same place a year apart is a record. One taken from roughly the same place is just another photograph.

Callum asked for one extra: every face of the kiosk, before it comes down, because [losing it was a close decision](/posts/what-do-we-keep) and he wants the record to be complete. It took a morning. Two drivers asked if I was from the newspaper.

## The north fold

Leela asked for something more exact. The north fold is where she is trying sample repairs, to find a concrete that matches the 1964 one, and she wanted it photographed square on, in flat light, with a grey card in the frame and the same exposure every time. That way a sample can be compared with the original concrete in a photograph as well as by eye. She'll explain the samples [later](/posts/forty-one-samples-of-concrete).

## Uncropped originals

Every photograph goes to the studio at full size, uncropped, with a note on what it shows and the number of the spot I stood on. A set also goes to Ríona, for the bus company's own record of the building.

I don't make a square version for the report and a wide one for the drawings. Whoever needs a crop takes it from the original. The edges matter more than you would think: the bin by the column, the pigeon on the downpipe, the bucket under valley nine. They are not the picture anyone wanted, but they are the record, and if someone needs these photographs for the next repair in thirty years, the whole frame will be there.

## A surface you can read

Concrete in flat light is a different material. On a sunny day, the Accordion is a shape. On a grey one, it is a surface you can read, and you can see that people made it by hand.

And the people who use a building know where to stand. On the second morning, a driver who has worked out of the station for twenty years showed me the spot where you can see all fourteen folds at once without stepping into the road. Every photographer before me had missed it. Every driver knows it. It is spot twelve.
