We flew to Copenhagen last Thursday for the first workshop at the long table in [the joinery](/posts/moving-into-the-joinery). Aoife ran it. Callum took notes, made the coffee, and kept the index cards away from Noir.

Twelve people came, all from Smeltehuset, the 1912 brass foundry in Nordvest that we turned into twenty-four flats for a housing cooperative two years ago. The flats are lived in and well liked. The casting hall, which was always meant to be the cooperative's shared workshop, is not. Two years on, it holds forty bicycles, a ping-pong table, a great many boxes, and a Christmas tree in a pot that nobody will admit to owning.

Maja Hald, the cooperative's chair, wrote to ask if we could help them decide what the hall was for. That is the only question Aoife ever asks, so we said yes.

## Two weeks of cards

Two weeks before, Aoife posted a pack of blank index cards to every flat with one instruction, translated by Jens: each time you wish you had somewhere to do something, write it on a card. One thing per card.

The twelve brought 163 cards between them. On Friday morning we laid them out on the long table and sorted them in silence. The piles that formed:

- **Fixing things:** bicycles (nine cards), a pram, a chair, a radio, a bicycle again.
- **Making things:** sewing, a bookshelf, a child's go-kart, Christmas decorations, "sanding a door without the whole flat knowing".
- **Gathering:** a birthday party for a six-year-old, a cooperative meeting that doesn't happen in someone's kitchen, a long table "like this one".
- **Keeping things:** mostly the bicycles again, and the boxes.

And, as always, the pile in the middle that nobody could explain. *Cast something.* *The chimney.* *Be alone for an hour.* And one card that just said *smell*, which turned out to mean that the casting hall still smells faintly of hot metal when it rains, and its writer, a retired engineer from the shipyard, did not want to lose that.

## Three cardboard halls

Elias had made a 1:50 model of the casting hall in grey card the week before: the walls, the roof trusses, the chimney, and nothing else. We gave the twelve three copies, split them into groups of four, and asked them to fit their piles into it.

Birthe arrived at two, as she does every Friday, with a thermos and a tin of biscuits. She looked at the three empty halls, nodded, and asked who wanted to learn the band saw.

## The band saw

Everyone did. Birthe teaches the band saw the way she taught it to her apprentices for thirty-four years: one person at a time, her hand on your shoulder, a push stick in yours, and the same three sentences in Danish, which Elias says mean *look at the line, not the blade; let the wood go at its own speed; your fingers are not in a hurry*. Each person cut one small block of pine for a workbench in their group's model. The two teenagers from the third floor cut four each and had to be asked to stop.

By four o'clock, every model had workbenches. None had a ping-pong table.

## Noir

Noir supervised from her blanket under the workbench, and came out only for Birthe's biscuits, which she gets first, and for one of the index cards, which fell off the table at about three. It was *fix the pram*. She carried it under the workbench and lay on it. The pram's owner said that seemed about right.

## What came out

The three models disagreed about almost everything except two things. All three put the long table under the chimney, where the furnace used to be, and all three moved the bicycles out of the hall and into the old pattern store next door, which had been empty since the cooperative moved in.

Everything else was arguable, and argued. The sewing went by the windows. The sanding went to the far end, behind a door.

Callum wrote it up on one page, the way he writes up [every building](/posts/the-schedule-is-one-page): what the hall is for, what goes where, and what nobody agreed on. Maja took it home to the cooperative's next meeting, which will be the first one held in the casting hall.

## What we'd change

Twelve is the right number for the long table. The two weeks of cards before the workshop made it. Two of the cards were in such small handwriting that nobody, including their writer, could read them; the cards were too small.

The building did a lot of the work. Before we had said a word, the twelve had sat at a long table among timber racks and watched someone cut a straight line in wood, and by the afternoon several of them were talking about their own hall as a workshop.

Birthe kept the third model. She says it's going on her windowsill.
