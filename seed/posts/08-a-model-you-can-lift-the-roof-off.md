Birthe has one name for every model we make, whatever it is, and she has used it since our first in the joinery, a test box with a folded lid. The Accordion's model is the first that deserves it, because the whole point of it is that you can lift the roof off and look inside.

## Why a model

We had drawings. The long section of the folded roof [hangs on the Dublin wall](/posts/why-were-called-long-section), and it explains how the roof meets the columns better than anything else we have. But a folded roof is hard to see on paper. A plan shows fourteen stripes. A section shows one zigzag. Ríona had worked under the roof for years and knew it well from below. Nobody had seen it from above except the pigeons and the people who built it.

So I asked for five Fridays in the back room.

## How it was built

At 1:50 the model is about eighty centimetres long. The base is an oak offcut Birthe chose from the racks, not one of the boards that have been seasoning since 2017, which nobody touches, and which I didn't ask about. The terrazzo floor is a print of Callum's survey, glued flat.

The roof took three tries. The first was card, scored and folded. It looked very good for four days, then the joinery had a damp week and the card remembered it. The second was too thick. The third is birch veneer under a millimetre thick, fourteen strips joined at the ridges and valleys, each fold bent over a jig.

The jig was Birthe's idea, and not a suggestion. "You make the jig," she said, "and then the jig makes the folds." I spent the whole of the second Friday making it, and the folds of the third took one afternoon.

The columns are balsa, cut on the band saw, which Birthe is teaching me to use properly, one Friday at a time. On top of each is a brass pin from a drawer of brass pins the joinery left behind. The roof sits on the pins. You lift it off with two hands, slowly, like a lid on a cake.

Inside, under the roof: the clock over the departures board, a paper disc stopped at twenty past four; the timetable boards; and the 1981 kiosk, as a separate block, because it was coming down in July and Ríona should be able to lift it out. Aoife had watched the concourse on and off that spring ([she watches before anyone draws](/posts/draw-the-people-before-the-building)), so the model has people in it too: forty small figures, standing where she saw them stand, most of them under the deepest fold.

## Lifting the roof off

In June the model flew to Dublin in a box on the seat next to me. Ríona came to the studio, and Helena put the model on the long table under the long section.

The planning drawings already showed the waiting room under the deepest fold, on Helena's argument and Aoife's spring of cards. Ríona had signed them, but said she couldn't see it in a plan, and if the model disagreed we wanted to know before the council did. So we tested three places: along the back wall at the north end, by the main door, and under the deepest fold. I'd cut each one out of acetate. With the roof off, all three looked fine. That's the problem with plans, and with models seen from above: from up there, everything fits.

Then we put the roof back, and looked in from the end at the height of a person, with a phone held down at the level of the figures' eyes. At the north end, the waiting room sat under the high part of a fold, in a draught, like a shed in a barn. By the door it was in the way of everyone. Under the deepest fold, the roof came down to meet it. The room sat in the lowest, most sheltered part of the building, exactly where Aoife's figures already were.

Ríona lifted the roof off herself, moved the acetate under the deepest fold, put the roof back, and looked again. She did that three times. Then she said, "There," and that was where it stayed.

Afterwards, Helena held a torch inside the acetate box, and the underside of the fold above it lit up like a lampshade. That is the drawing we did next.

## The column

One Friday in May, a balsa column was drying on the end of the workbench. Noir, who lives under the workbench, took it to her blanket. We found it an hour later, with one end considerably shorter. Birthe cut a new one in about four minutes and said the dog had good taste in timber. Noir has her own account of this.

## What the model taught me

- **A model you can open is a model people touch.** Nobody has ever moved a rectangle on one of my drawings. Ríona moved the acetate three times.
- **Look from eye height, not from above.** From above, every plan works.
- **Make the jig first.**
- **Glue nothing you're still deciding.** The kiosk is still a separate block.
